\section{The signaling form}\label{sec:signaling}

Everything so far describes the observer from outside: the record at scale $x$ is
$u(t, x) = (\mu_{0,x} * f)(t)$, a convolution reaching back into the raw past. The
embodiment principle of \S\ref{sec:measurement} demands more --- the past is gone, and
whatever of it influences the present must be carried in the observer's own state. This
section shows that the demand is met, in the strongest sense: the field can be evolved
out of its present state alone, with the signal entering only where it physically
enters, at the fine-scale end. In the language of classical PDE this is a
\emph{signaling} problem rather than a Cauchy problem --- the data live on the spatial
boundary for all time, not on an initial time-slice --- the model being a rod driven at
its end:
\[
  \partial_t u \;=\; A\,u, \qquad u(t, 0) = f(t), \qquad u(0, x) = 0,
\]
with $A$ an operator in the scale variable alone (Figure~\ref{fig:signaling}). Scale
becomes space, time the evolution variable, and the memory line a medium whose interior
transport law is $A$. Theorem~4 of \sscite{fagerstrom2005temporal} solved this in the
homogeneous case, with $A = D^{1/\alpha}_{x,-}$ the right-sided Liouville derivative
(\sscite{samko1993fractional}) --- a \emph{translation-invariant} operator in $x$,
available because the semigroup's scale axis carries an additive structure. The
hemigroup removes that structure, and with it the operator; this section constructs the
general inversion from what survives.

Throughout we work in the canonical gauge, so that $\mu_{0,x}$ is the law of $x\,T_1$ with
$\Ex[e^{-sT_1}] = H(s)$, $H := e^{-F}$; we write $\hat g(s) := \int_0^\infty e^{-st}g(t)\,dt$
for the Laplace transform in the time variable,
$\tilde g(z) := \int_0^\infty x^{z-1} g(x)\,dx$ for the Mellin transform in the scale
variable, $\theta := x\,\partial_x$ for the Euler operator, and
$(I^z f)(t) := \tfrac{1}{\Gamma(z)}\int_0^t (t-r)^{z-1} f(r)\,dr$ for the Riemann--Liouville
integral of complex order $z$, $\operatorname{Re} z > 0$ (\sscite{samko1993fractional}).

What survives is dilation structure, and it is enough. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:main-characterization} the Laplace-domain profiles of the field,
\[
  \hat u(s, x) = \hat f(s)\, H(sx),
\]
are \emph{dilates of a single profile} $H$, with the time-eigenvalue $s$ appearing as
the dilation parameter; and $\partial_t$ acts on the causal mode at rate $s$ by
multiplication by $s$. So the sought operator must read off where a profile sits on its
dilation orbit and return that position as a number: $A[H(s\,\cdot)] = s\,H(s\,\cdot)$
for every $s$. Operators diagonal on dilation orbits are the subject of multiplicative
harmonic analysis, so $A$ lives in the \textbf{Mellin calculus} --- the
multiplicative-group counterpart of the Wendel step of \S\ref{sec:representation}, now
run in reverse: there an operator was given and its kernel extracted; here the spectral
data are prescribed and the operator must be synthesized. Covariance fixes its shape
outright --- under $(t,x) \mapsto (\sigma t, \sigma x)$ an inversion must scale like
$\partial_t$, and in the canonical gauge $x$ carries the dimension of time while the
Euler operator $\theta = x\partial_x$ is dimensionless, so $A = x^{-1}B(\theta)$ ---
and the eigenvalue demand then determines the symbol with no freedom left: on
transforms the weight $x$ shifts the Mellin argument by one, forcing $B(-z) =
\tilde H(z+1)/\tilde H(z)$, the recursion of the profile's Mellin data. The theorem of
this section (Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}) makes all of this precise: \emph{the
signaling problem has a covariant inversion; the scale-space field solves it; and no
other covariant Mellin operator does}. What stands between the ansatz and the theorem
is analytic honesty --- the symbol is a ratio and may have poles, so the strip, the
transforms, and a careful functional calculus must be built first; that is the business
of the lemmas below.

The transforms of the profile and of the field are the section's working objects, and
they need a strip of the complex plane to live on; the standing hypothesis is what
opens one. The strip must moreover have width greater than one, because the equation
shifts the Mellin argument by exactly one --- differentiation in time and the weight
$x$ are the same shift seen from two sides --- which is where the condition $z_* > 1$
comes from.

\begin{definition}[the standing hypothesis (H)]\label{def:standing-hypothesis}
An admissible exponent $F$ satisfies the \emph{standing hypothesis} (H) if
$F(\infty) = \infty$ and
\[
  z_* \;:=\; \sup\bigl\{\, \zeta > 0 :\ \Ex\bigl[T_1^{-\zeta}\bigr] < \infty \,\bigr\} \;>\; 1 .
\]
\end{definition}

Both clauses are conditions at the zero-delay end of the kernels, and
Lemmas~\ref{lem:standing-kernel-readings} and~\ref{lem:standing-levy-reading} below make the
readings precise: the first clause says exactly that the kernels put no atom at zero delay, is
equivalent to $b_0 > 0$ or $\int_0^\infty k(t)\,t^{-1}dt = \infty$, and is automatic for every
admissible $F \not\equiv 0$ --- so all of (H)'s bite is in the second clause --- while the
second implies $\int_0^\infty e^{-F(s)}\,ds = \Ex[T_1^{-1}] < \infty$, the converse failing
only at the endpoint $z_* = 1$, an endpoint genuinely reached: the Gamma family at
$\gamma = 1$ has $z_* = 1$ with $\Ex[T_1^{-1}] = \infty$. The second clause holds with
$z_* = \infty$ whenever $b_0 > 0$, for the stable family, and for the Bessel-K family of
Proposition~\ref{prop:bessel-family}; for the Gamma family $z_* = \gamma$, so (H) requires
$\gamma > 1$ there, and $\gamma \le 1$ is among the open problems of
\S\ref{sec:conclusions}. The rate is what Lemma~\ref{lem:zstar-log-growth} computes in general:
$z_* = \lim F(s)/\log s$, which is $k(0^+)$ when $b_0 = 0$ and $\infty$ otherwise, so (H) reads:
drift, or catalogue height above one at the origin. The construction extends below $z_* = 1$ by
meromorphic continuation, which we do not pursue.

% shared with blueprint lem:standing-kernel-readings
\begin{lemma}[kernel readings of the standing hypothesis]\label{lem:standing-kernel-readings}
In the canonical gauge, with $T_1$ as in Definition~\ref{def:standing-hypothesis}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $F(\infty) = \infty$ if and only if the kernels have no atom at zero delay:
$\mu_{0,x}(\{0\}) = \Pr[T_1 = 0] = 0$ for every $x > 0$.
\item Suppose $F(\infty) = \infty$. Then $\int_0^\infty e^{-F(s)}\,ds = \Ex[T_1^{-1}]$ in
$[0,\infty]$; consequently $z_* > 1$ implies $\int_0^\infty e^{-F(s)}\,ds < \infty$, and
$\int_0^\infty e^{-F(s)}\,ds < \infty$ implies $z_* \ge 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) $s \mapsto H(s) := \Ex[e^{-sT_1}]$ decreases to $\Pr[T_1 = 0]$ as $s \to \infty$: the
integrand $e^{-sT_1}$ is dominated by the constant $1$ and converges pointwise to the indicator
of $\{T_1 = 0\}$, so dominated convergence gives $H(s) \to \Pr[T_1=0]$. Since $H(s) = e^{-F(s)}$,
$F(\infty) = \infty$ iff $H(s) \to 0$ iff $\Pr[T_1 = 0] = 0$. For general $x > 0$, $\mu_{0,x}$ is
the law of $xT_1$ (the canonical gauge), and multiplication by $x \ne 0$ carries $\{0\}$ to
itself and nowhere else, so $\mu_{0,x}(\{0\}) = \Pr[xT_1 = 0] = \Pr[T_1 = 0]$ for every $x > 0$;
the case $x=1$ already gives the stated equivalence, and every other $x$ agrees with it.

(2) By Tonelli, $\int_0^\infty e^{-F(s)}\,ds = \int_0^\infty \Ex[e^{-sT_1}]\,ds =
\Ex\bigl[\int_0^\infty e^{-sT_1}\,ds\bigr] = \Ex[T_1^{-1}]$ in $[0,\infty]$, the inner integral
$\int_0^\infty e^{-sT_1}ds = T_1^{-1}$ being licensed pointwise because $T_1 > 0$ almost surely
by~(1). For the two consequences, the finiteness set
$\{\zeta > 0 : \Ex[T_1^{-\zeta}] < \infty\}$ is downward closed --- $t^{-\zeta'} \le
t^{-\zeta} + 1$ for $t > 0$ and $\zeta' \le \zeta$, so a finite $\zeta$-th moment bounds every
smaller one up to an additive unit of mass --- so $z_* > 1$ puts a real $\zeta > 1$ in the set,
and hence $1$ in it too, giving $\Ex[T_1^{-1}] < \infty$; conversely if $\Ex[T_1^{-1}] < \infty$
then $1$ is itself a member of the set whose supremum is $z_*$, so $z_* \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint lem:standing-levy-reading
\begin{lemma}[Lévy-data reading; the first clause is automatic]\label{lem:standing-levy-reading}
Let $F$ be admissible with representation (7.1). Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $F(\infty) = \infty$ if and only if $b_0 > 0$ or $\int_0^\infty k(t)\,t^{-1}dt = \infty$
--- i.e.\ the exponent carries drift or infinite Lévy mass.
\item If $F \not\equiv 0$ then $F(\infty) = \infty$; within the admissible class the standing
hypothesis therefore reduces to its second clause, $z_* > 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) ($\Leftarrow$) If $b_0 > 0$ then $F(s) \ge b_0 s \to \infty$. If $\int_0^\infty k(t)t^{-1}dt =
\infty$, monotone convergence in (7.1) --- $1 - e^{-st}$ increases pointwise to $1$ on
$(0,\infty)$ as $s \to \infty$ --- gives $F(s) \to b_0\cdot\infty + \int_0^\infty k(t)t^{-1}dt =
\infty$. ($\Rightarrow$) Contrapositive: if $b_0 = 0$ and $\int_0^\infty k(t)t^{-1}dt =: M <
\infty$, the same monotone limit gives $F(s)$ increasing to $M < \infty$, so
$F(\infty) \ne \infty$.

(2) If $k \not\equiv 0$, nonincreasing $k$ that is positive somewhere is bounded below by that
value on the interval below it: pick $t_0$ with $k(t_0) > 0$, then $k(t) \ge k(t_0)$ for
$t \in (0,t_0]$, so $\int_0^{t_0} k(t)t^{-1}dt \ge k(t_0)\int_0^{t_0}t^{-1}dt = \infty$, and (1)
gives $F(\infty) = \infty$. If $k \equiv 0$ and $F \not\equiv 0$, (7.1) reduces to $F(s) = b_0 s$
with $b_0 > 0$, and (1) again gives $F(\infty) = \infty$.
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint lem:zstar-log-growth
\begin{lemma}[the log-growth reading of $z_*$]\label{lem:zstar-log-growth}
Let $F \not\equiv 0$ be admissible, with $(b_0, k)$ as in (7.1). Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The limit $\lim_{s\to\infty} F(s)/\log s$ exists in $[0,\infty]$ and equals $z_*$.
\item The limit is $\infty$ if $b_0 > 0$, and equals $k(0^+) := \sup_{t>0} k(t)$ if $b_0 = 0$
(finite or infinite).
\item Consequently, (H) holds if and only if $b_0 > 0$ or $k(0^+) > 1$.
\item $z_*$ is homogeneous of degree one on the admissible cone: $z_*(cF) = c\,z_*(F)$ for every
$c > 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $F \not\equiv 0$ is admissible, Lemma~\ref{lem:standing-levy-reading}(2) gives
$F(\infty) = \infty$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:standing-kernel-readings}(1) turns that into $T_1 > 0$
almost surely, so $\Ex[T_1^{-\zeta}]$ is an honest integral over $(0,\infty)$ for every
$\zeta > 0$.

\textbf{The exponent's slope.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-kernel}, $F'(s) = b_0 + \int_0^\infty
e^{-st}k(t)\,dt$, so $B(s) := sF'(s) = b_0 s + \int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,k(u/s)\,du$ after the
substitution $u = st$. For $0 < s_1 \le s_2$: $b_0 s_1 \le b_0 s_2$, and $k$ nonincreasing gives
$k(u/s_1) \le k(u/s_2)$ pointwise, since $u/s_1 \ge u/s_2$; so $B$ is nondecreasing --- no appeal
to Lemma~\ref{lem:selfdecomposable-exponents} or to $B$ being a Bernstein function as a class,
only to the explicit formula and the definition of nonincreasing. And since $k(u/s)$ increases
pointwise to $k(0^+)$ as $s \to \infty$, monotone convergence gives
$B(\infty) = b_0\cdot\infty + k(0^+)\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,du$: $\infty$ if $b_0 > 0$, and $k(0^+)$
if $b_0 = 0$. That is clause~(2).

(1) $G(u) := F(e^u)$ has $G'(u) = e^u F'(e^u) = B(e^u)$ nondecreasing by the above, so $G$ is
convex in $u = \log s$. A convex function's difference quotient $G(u)/u$ increases, past any zero
of $u$, to its terminal slope, so
$L := \lim_{s\to\infty} F(s)/\log s = \lim_{u\to\infty} G(u)/u = \lim_{s\to\infty} B(s)$ exists in
$[0,\infty]$, with value given by clause~(2). To identify $L$ with $z_*$: by Tonelli,
$\Gamma(\zeta)\,\Ex[T_1^{-\zeta}] = \int_0^\infty s^{\zeta-1}e^{-F(s)}\,ds$ for every $\zeta > 0$,
the exchange licensed exactly as in Lemma~\ref{lem:standing-kernel-readings}(2), now at general
$\zeta$ rather than at $\zeta = 1$. For $0 < \zeta < L$, eventually
$F(s) \ge (\zeta+\varepsilon)\log s$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$, so
$e^{-F(s)} \le s^{-\zeta-\varepsilon}$ eventually and the integral converges; for $\zeta > L$,
eventually $F(s) \le (\zeta - \varepsilon)\log s$, so the integrand is eventually
$\ge s^{\varepsilon - 1}$ and the integral diverges. Hence
$\{\zeta > 0 : \Ex[T_1^{-\zeta}] < \infty\}$ contains every $\zeta < L$ and excludes every
$\zeta > L$, so its supremum, $z_*$, is $L$.

(3) is~(1)--(2) read against (H)'s two clauses. The first is automatic here by
Lemma~\ref{lem:standing-levy-reading}(2), so (H) reduces to $z_* > 1$, which by~(2) is exactly
$b_0 > 0$ or $k(0^+) > 1$ --- an equality throughout, so with no boundary subtlety.

(4) $cF$ is admissible by Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible-cone}, with data $(cb_0, ck)$, so
$k_{cF}(0^+) = c\,k(0^+)$ and $(cF)$'s drift is $cb_0$; applying~(1)--(2) to $cF$ in place of $F$
gives $z_*(cF) = c\,z_*(F)$, whether $b_0 > 0$ or $b_0 = 0$.
\end{proof}

The threshold $1$ is not an artefact of one estimate. It arises three separate times, for
three different reasons: in the convergence of the Mellin integral of the delayed field
(Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals}), in the application of that lemma at $z - 1$
in Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2), and in the convergence of $\int_\rho^t (t-r)^{z-2}dr$
inside the same proof --- three obligations, one endpoint. And it is where the cone's extreme
rays sit: every extreme ray with $b_0 = 0$ has $F(s) \sim \log s$, hence $z_* = 1$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:extreme-rays}; also Lemma~\ref{lem:zstar-log-growth}(2), since
$k(0^+) = 1$ on every Dickman ray), so those rays lie exactly \emph{on} the boundary of
(H). The correspondence is not exact in either direction --- the pure delay is an extreme ray
and satisfies (H) outright, having $b_0 > 0$, and the Gamma family at $\gamma \le 1$ fails
(H) without being extreme --- but the coincidence of the three obligations at the extreme
rays' abscissa marks the boundary of (H) as characteristic of the theory rather than of any
one argument.

Figure~\ref{fig:admissible-cone} locates the cut in the cone of \S\ref{sec:characterization},
and the location says what the hypothesis costs. Nothing from the preceding sections is given
up: an admissible family failing (H) is still a causal scale space --- the representation, the
cascade, and the cone geometry all stand --- and what is withheld is only this section's
reading of it: the covariant inversion needs a Mellin strip of width greater than one, and the
excluded members --- the slowest media, those whose exponents grow at most logarithmically
(Lemma~\ref{lem:zstar-log-growth}(1)) --- do not open one. The shape of the excluded set
follows from homogeneity (Lemma~\ref{lem:zstar-log-growth}(4)). The Thorin condition selects
rays: complete monotonicity of $k$ survives positive multiples, so the subclass is itself a
cone. (H) instead cuts across rays: a ray of finite $z_*$ enters (H) beyond the threshold
multiple $1/z_*$, so the excluded set is a collar around the apex $F = 0$, pinching to nothing
wherever $z_* = \infty$ --- every exponent with drift, the stable and Bessel-K families ---
and reaching exactly the unit point of each Dickman ray. The two cuts are independent:
multiples of a single Dickman ray fall on both sides of (H) while never being Thorin --- a
step is not completely monotone --- and the Gamma family falls on both sides while always
being Thorin.

% shared with blueprint lem:mellin-data
\begin{lemma}[Mellin data of the profile]\label{lem:mellin-data}
Under (H), for $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$,
\[
  \tilde H(z) \;=\; \Gamma(z)\;\Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z}\bigr],
\]
with $|\tilde H(c + i\tau)| \le \Ex[T_1^{-c}]\,|\Gamma(c+i\tau)|$ for $0 < c < z_*$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Tonelli,
\[
  \tilde H(z) = \int_0^\infty s^{z-1}\,\Ex[e^{-sT_1}]\,ds
  = \Ex\Bigl[\int_0^\infty s^{z-1}e^{-sT_1}ds\Bigr]
  = \Ex[T_1^{-z}]\,\Gamma(z),
\]
the inner integral being the Gamma integral at rate $T_1$. The exchange is legitimate because
the same computation run on absolute values gives $\Gamma(c)\,\Ex[T_1^{-c}]$ with
$c = \operatorname{Re} z$, finite exactly when $c < z_*$ --- so the strip condition is
characteristic of the exchange, not merely sufficient for it. The first clause of (H) is used
here, and only here: it gives $T_1 > 0$ almost surely, without which the identity is false
rather than merely unproved --- at an atom $t = 0$ the inner integral $\int_0^\infty s^{c-1}ds$
diverges, while $\Ex[T_1^{-c}]$, an integral over $(0,\infty)$, does not see the atom. Since
$|T_1^{-z}| = T_1^{-\operatorname{Re} z}$, the identity also gives the stated bound.
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint lem:mellin-vertical
\begin{lemma}[the profile's transform is integrable on vertical lines]\label{lem:mellin-vertical}
Under (H), for every $c \in (0, z_*)$ the function $\tau \mapsto \tilde H(c + i\tau)$ is
absolutely integrable on $\RR$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$|\tilde H(c+i\tau)| \le \Ex[T_1^{-c}]\,|\Gamma(c+i\tau)|$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data}, the
first factor being finite because $c < z_*$. For the second, quadratic decay suffices, and
quadratic decay is the functional equation twice. Euler's integral gives
$|\Gamma(\sigma+i\tau)| \le \Gamma(\sigma)$ for $\sigma > 0$, the imaginary part only rotating
the integrand; and $\Gamma(z+2) = (z+1)z\Gamma(z)$ with $|z|, |z+1| \ge |\tau|$ --- both have
imaginary part $\tau$ --- gives $|\Gamma(c+i\tau)|\,\tau^2 \le \Gamma(c+2)$. Adding them,
\[
  |\Gamma(c+i\tau)|\,(1+\tau^2) \;\le\; \Gamma(c) + \Gamma(c+2),
\]
and $(1+\tau^2)^{-1}$ is integrable on the line. (The classical asymptotic
$|\Gamma(c+i\tau)| \sim \sqrt{2\pi}\,|\tau|^{c-1/2}e^{-\pi|\tau|/2}$ is stronger, but it needs
Stirling in the complex plane and nothing below uses it.)
\end{proof}

The stage set, the ansatz of the lead is made honest in three steps: the symbol exists
as a meromorphic function, the functional calculus $x^{-1}B(\theta)$ is given a
definition that survives the symbol's poles, and the profiles are verified to be its
eigenfunctions.

% shared with blueprint lem:inversion-symbol
\begin{lemma}[the inversion symbol]\label{lem:inversion-symbol}
Under (H), $\tilde H$ is analytic on the strip $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ and does not
vanish identically there. Define the \emph{inversion symbol}
\[
  B(-z) \;:=\; \frac{\tilde H(z+1)}{\tilde H(z)}
  \;=\; z\,\frac{\Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z-1}\bigr]}{\Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z}\bigr]},
  \qquad 0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_* - 1 .
\]
Then $B$ is meromorphic on that strip, with poles only at the isolated zeros of $\tilde H$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Analyticity is the identity of Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data} read backwards rather than a
separate estimate: $m(z) := \Ex[T_1^{-z}] = \Ex[e^{-z\log T_1}]$ is the moment-generating
function of $-\log T_1$, analytic on the interior of the set of exponents where the
corresponding real exponential is integrable --- which is $(0, z_*)$, the strip condition
verbatim --- and $\Gamma$ contributes the rest. $\tilde H$ is not identically zero: both
factors are strictly positive at real $z \in (0, z_*)$. Hence its zeros are isolated, and the
quotient $\tilde H(z+1)/\tilde H(z)$ --- which is $z\,\Ex[T_1^{-z-1}]/\Ex[T_1^{-z}]$ by
$\Gamma(z+1) = z\Gamma(z)$ --- is meromorphic with poles only there.
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint def:inversion-operator — the pointer to the blueprint's split
% eigenfunction node is adapted to Theorem (1) below.
\begin{definition}[the inversion operator]\label{def:inversion-operator}
Fix $c \in (0, z_* - 1)$. For $g$ such that $\tilde g$ is defined on the line
$\operatorname{Re} z = c$ and $B(-z)\tilde g(z)$ is absolutely integrable there, set
\[
  \bigl(A g\bigr)(x) \;:=\; \frac{1}{x}\cdot \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)} x^{-z}\, B(-z)\,\tilde g(z)\,dz
  \;=\; \Bigl(\frac{1}{x}\,B(\theta)\,g\Bigr)(x),
\]
the second expression being the functional-calculus reading, the Euler operator having Mellin
symbol $-z$.
\end{definition}

The second equality is an assertion and not a rewriting, and it is worth being explicit about
what it asserts: $B(\theta)$ is a functional calculus for a symbol with poles, so what the
display says is that there \emph{exists} a function $h$ on $(0,\infty)$ whose Mellin transform is
$B(-z)\tilde g(z)$ on the line, and that the contour integral computes $h(x)/x$. Absolute
integrability on the line is exactly the hypothesis under which the inversion integral recovers
such an $h$. Given $h$, the rest is elementary and is Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-operator-action};
in particular the transform-level form is $\widetilde{A g}(z) = B(1 - z)\,\tilde g(z - 1)$,
wherever the identity defining $h$ holds pointwise. Every application below \emph{exhibits} its
$h$ rather than inferring it --- for the profiles $g = H(s\,\cdot)$ it is $h(x) = s\,x\,H(sx)$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:profile-eigenfunction} --- so the cited inversion theorem is available but never
spent.

% shared with blueprint lem:inversion-operator-action
\begin{lemma}[the operator computes the realising function]\label{lem:inversion-operator-action}
Fix $c > 0$ and $g$, and let $h$ be a function on $(0,\infty)$ such that
\[
  \tilde h(z) = B(-z)\,\tilde g(z)
\]
for almost every $z$ on the line $\operatorname{Re} z = c$, with both $\tilde h$ and the
inversion integral absolutely convergent there. Then
\[
  (A g)(x) = \frac{h(x)}{x} \quad\text{at every } x > 0 \text{ where } h \text{ is continuous},
\qquad
  \widetilde{A g}(z) = \tilde h(z-1) \quad\text{for every } z .
\]
In particular $\widetilde{A g}(z) = B(1-z)\,\tilde g(z-1)$ wherever the first display holds
pointwise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The integrand of the contour integral agrees almost everywhere on the line with $\tilde h$, so
the integral is the inverse Mellin transform of $\tilde h$ at height $c$; under the two
convergence hypotheses that recovers $h(x)$ at every point of continuity, which is the first
display. The second is then the Mellin transform of $x^{-1}h(x)$, i.e.\ $\tilde h$ with its
argument shifted by one. (That the identity on the line can only be asked for almost
everywhere is forced: where $\tilde H$ vanishes the product $B(-z)\tilde g(z)$ has no value
while $\tilde h(z)$ does; the zeros are isolated by Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}, hence
meet the line in a null set, and the inversion integral does not see them.)
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint lem:profile-eigenfunction
\begin{lemma}[the profiles are eigenfunctions]\label{lem:profile-eigenfunction}
Assume (H), let $c \in (0, z_*-1)$ and $s > 0$. Then the dilate $H(s\,\cdot)$ lies in the domain of
Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator} --- its realising function is $h(x) = s\,x\,H(sx)$,
exhibited --- and
\[
  A\bigl[H(s\,\cdot)\bigr](x) \;=\; s\,H(sx), \qquad x > 0 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\widetilde{H(s\cdot)}(z) = s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$ and $\widetilde{s\,x\,H(sx)}(z) = s^{-z}\tilde
H(z+1)$, the second because the weight $x$ shifts the Mellin argument by one. Their ratio is
$B(-z)$ wherever $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$, which is almost every point of the line. The transform of
$h$ converges there by Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data} and its inversion integral by
Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-vertical}, both at height $c+1 \in (1, z_*)$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-operator-action} applies and gives $A[H(s\cdot)](x) = h(x)/x =
s\,H(sx)$.
\end{proof}

The image of a profile is the same profile, scaled; so the profiles lie in the domain of every
power of $A$, and by induction $A^n[H(s\,\cdot)] = s^n\,H(s\,\cdot)$, each iterate realised by
the exhibited $h(x) = s^n x\,H(sx)$. This is what the jet construction of \S\ref{sec:jet}
iterates.

The first verification concerned the operator on single modes; the second concerns the
field itself, and it is the section's central identity: what the memory line actually
stores.

\begin{lemma}[the memory line stores fractional integrals]\label{lem:memory-fractional-integrals}
Under (H), let $f \in \Xz$ be causal and $u(\cdot, x) := \Phi_{0,x} f$. Then for every $t > 0$
and $1 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$,
\[
  \widetilde{u(t, \cdot)}(z) \;=\; \int_0^\infty x^{z-1}\, u(t, x)\,dx
  \;=\; \tilde H(z)\,\bigl(I^{z} f\bigr)(t),
\]
absolutely convergent. If moreover $f \in \core$, the same holds for $\partial_t u$ --- the
$\Xz$-derivative of Lemma~\ref{lem:delay-core}, i.e.\ $u(\cdot, x)$ is the primitive of
$\Ex[f'(\cdot - xT_1)]$ --- with $I^z f$ replaced by $I^z f' = I^{z-1} f$; and in that case the
identity extends to the wider strip $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In the canonical gauge $u(t, x) = \Ex\bigl[f(t - x T_1)\bigr]$. Tonelli applies, since for
$c = \operatorname{Re} z > 1$,
$\Ex[T_1^{-c}]\int_0^t y^{c-1}|f(t-y)|\,dy \le \Ex[T_1^{-c}]\, t^{c-1}\|f\|_1 < \infty$, and
gives
\[
  \int_0^\infty x^{z-1}\,\Ex\bigl[f(t - xT_1)\bigr]\,dx
  \;=\; \Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z}\bigr]\int_0^t y^{z-1} f(t - y)\,dy
  \;=\; \Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z}\bigr]\,\Gamma(z)\,(I^z f)(t),
\]
which is the claim by Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data}. (For bounded $f$ the inner integral
converges for every $c > 0$, which is the wider strip.) For $f \in \core$, $f$ is bounded ---
$f(0) = 0$ and $f' \in \Xz$ give $\|f\|_\infty \le \|f'\|_1$ --- so the weight $x^{z-1}$ is then
integrable at the origin on its own, which is where that extension is used. Also for
$f \in \core$: $u(\cdot,x) = \mu_{0,x} * f$ and $f = \mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)} * f'$, so
$u(\cdot,x) = \mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)} * (\mu_{0,x} * f')$ --- that is, $u(\cdot,x)$ is the
primitive of $\Ex[f'(\cdot - xT_1)]$, which is what $\partial_t u = \Ex[f'(\cdot - xT_1)]$
asserts in $\Xz$. (It is \emph{not} an assertion about pointwise derivatives, and would be
false as one: $f'$ exists only a.e., and $\mu_{0,x} * f'$ is an $L^1$ function with no
pointwise values to be a derivative at.) Finally $I^z f' = I^{z-1} f$: substituting
$f(r) = \int_0^r f'$ and exchanging the order of integration over $0 < \rho \le r \le t$
leaves $\int_\rho^t (t-r)^{z-2}dr = (t-\rho)^{z-1}/(z-1)$, and
$\Gamma(z) = (z-1)\Gamma(z-1)$; the semigroup identity $I^{z-1}I^1 = I^z$ is not needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the core supplies]\label{rem:core-accounting}
Of the three conditions defining $\core$ the proofs use exactly three consequences and no
others: boundedness, which is what widens the strip; that $f$ is the primitive of $f'$, which
is what the derivative clause asserts; and $f \in \Xz$ itself, needed for the Laplace form of
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) and \emph{not} implied by the other two, a primitive of an
$L^1$ function lying in $L^1$ only when $\int_0^\infty f' = 0$. Density and invariance under
$\shift_r$ and $\Phi_{x,y}$ --- the substance of Lemma~\ref{lem:delay-core} --- are not used
here.
\end{remark}

% Verbatim from the blueprint's rem:embodiment.
\begin{remark}[reading of Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals}; embodiment]\label{rem:embodiment}
Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals} gives the motivation of
\sscite{fagerstrom2005temporal}, \S5, an exact form: at each instant $t$, the Mellin analysis
of the memory line holds precisely the analytic family of Riemann--Liouville integrals
$\{(I^z f)(t)\}$ of the \emph{past} signal, weighted by the negative delay-moments
$\Ex[T_1^{-z}]$. The observer's embodiment of its past is fractional integration, with the
admissible weightings classified by Theorem~\ref{thm:main-characterization}.
\end{remark}

It remains to show that nothing else could have been built. The uniqueness argument
needs an arena --- what counts as a competitor --- and one rigidity lemma.

% shared with blueprint lem:symbol-rigidity
\begin{lemma}[rigidity of the symbol]\label{lem:symbol-rigidity}
Assume (H), and let $B_1, B_2$ satisfy
\[
  B_1(-z)\,\tilde H(z) \;=\; B_2(-z)\,\tilde H(z),
  \qquad 0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_* - 1 .
\]
Then $B_1 = B_2$ as meromorphic functions on that strip: they agree on a punctured
neighbourhood of every point of it. Where in addition both are continuous --- in particular at
any point that is a pole of neither --- they agree there too.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Where $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$, cancel it. By Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol} the zeros of
$\tilde H$ are isolated, so that cancellation is available on a punctured neighbourhood of
\emph{every} point of the strip, the zeros included; hence $B_1$ and $B_2$ agree near every
point, the point itself excepted. If both are continuous at such a point, the punctured
neighbourhood determines the value there and the agreement extends to it.
\end{proof}

% shared with blueprint def:covariant-mellin-class
\begin{definition}[the covariant Mellin class]\label{def:covariant-mellin-class}
Fix $c \in (0, z_* - 1)$. An operator $A'$ belongs to the \emph{covariant Mellin class} at
height $c$ if there is a function $B'(-z)$ on the line $\operatorname{Re} z = c$ such that $A'$
is of the form $x^{-1}B'(\theta)$ in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}: every
$g$ in its domain has a realising function $h$ with $\tilde h(z) = B'(-z)\tilde g(z)$ at every
point of the line where $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$, and $A'g = h/x$. The weight $x^{-1}$ makes every
member scale like $\partial_t$ under $(t,x) \mapsto (\sigma t, \sigma x)$, which is the
covariance (A8) requires of an inversion --- the class is the covariant one.
\end{definition}

% shared with blueprint lem:symbol-uniqueness
\begin{lemma}[uniqueness of the symbol]\label{lem:symbol-uniqueness}
If two operators of the form $x^{-1}B_1(\theta)$ and $x^{-1}B_2(\theta)$ both satisfy
$A[H(s\,\cdot)] = s\,H(s\,\cdot)$ for some $s > 0$, at every height $c \in (0, z_* - 1)$ --- that
is, at every such $c$ both are of that form on $H(s\,\cdot)$ at height $c$ and both send it to
$s\,H(s\,\cdot)$ --- then $B_1 = B_2$ on the strip. Hence $A$ is the unique inversion within the
covariant Mellin class (Definition~\ref{def:covariant-mellin-class}).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
``Of the form $x^{-1}B_i(\theta)$'' is the hypothesis that each operator has a realising
function in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}. Fix a height
$c \in (0, z_* - 1)$. Both operators send $H(s\,\cdot)$ to $s\,H(s\,\cdot)$, so on $(0,\infty)$
their realising functions at height $c$ agree, since $x^{-1}h_1 = x^{-1}h_2$ on $(0,\infty)$ is
$h_1 = h_2$; call the common value $h$. Then
$B_1(-z)\,\tilde g(z) = \tilde h(z) = B_2(-z)\,\tilde g(z)$ at every point of the line
$\operatorname{Re} z = c$ where the identity holds, and $\tilde g(z) = s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$, so
cancelling the nonvanishing factor $s^{-z}$ gives $B_1(-z)\tilde H(z) = B_2(-z)\tilde H(z)$ at
every point of the line where $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$, and trivially at its zeros, where both sides
vanish. As $c$ ranges over $(0, z_* - 1)$ the lines fill the strip, which is the hypothesis of
Lemma~\ref{lem:symbol-rigidity}, and that lemma concludes. (This is where the hypothesis at
every height is spent: with the identity on a single line one would need $B_1, B_2$ meromorphic
on the strip and the identity theorem to reach the rest of it, and neither is assumed.) Two
economies are worth recording, since both tools are natural to reach for and neither is needed:
no injectivity of the inverse Mellin transform, the shared realising function carrying the
information that injectivity would otherwise have to recover; and a single dilation rather than
all $s > 0$, the dilate contributing only the factor $s^{-z}$, which never vanishes.
\end{proof}

The pieces assemble. Recall the problem: evolve the field in present time over the
memory line, the signal entering as boundary data,
\[
  \partial_t u = A\,u, \qquad u(t,0) = f(t), \qquad u(0,x) = 0 .
\]
The theorem delivers, over the covariant Mellin class: the inversion exists and has
the field's Laplace profiles as eigenfunctions (1); the scale-space field solves the
problem, the equation holding mode-by-mode in the Laplace variable and as an identity
of Mellin transforms in the scale variable (2); and the inversion is unique (3) ---
the \emph{operator}, that is, not yet the solution. What ``solves'' means, and the
sense in which the problem is genuinely \emph{solved}, is settled after the
theorem: Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution} names the notion of solution that
clause~(2) informally reads off, Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-wellposed} adds
\emph{solution}-uniqueness, and Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-hadamard} completes
Hadamard's demands.

% shared with blueprint thm:signaling-form
\begin{theorem}[Theorem 4$'$: the signaling form]\label{thm:signaling-form}
Assume (H) and work in the canonical gauge. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Eigenfunctions.)} For every $s > 0$ the profile $H(s\,\cdot)$ lies in the domain
of Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator} for every $c \in (0, z_*-1)$, and
\[
  A\bigl[H(s\,\cdot)\bigr](x) \;=\; s\, H(sx), \qquad x > 0 .
\]
\item \emph{(The field solves the signaling problem.)} Let $f \in \core$ and
$u(\cdot, x) := \Phi_{0,x} f$. Then $u$ is causal in $t$ with $u(\cdot, x) \to f$ in $\Xz$ as
$x \downarrow 0$, and
\begin{itemize}
\item Laplace form: for every $s > 0$, $A\bigl[\hat u(s, \cdot)\bigr] = s\,\hat u(s, \cdot)$,
with $\hat u(s, \zp) = \hat f(s)$;
\item time-domain (Mellin) form: for every $t > 0$ and every $z$ with
$1 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ and $\tilde H(z-1) \ne 0$ --- all of the strip off an isolated
set, Remark~\ref{rem:poles} ---
\[
  \widetilde{\partial_t u(t, \cdot)}(z) \;=\; B(1 - z)\;\widetilde{u(t, \cdot)}(z - 1),
\]
both transforms absolutely convergent; that is, $\partial_t u = A\,u$ read through the Mellin
transform in $x$.
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{(Uniqueness.)} $A$ is the unique operator in the covariant Mellin class
(Definition~\ref{def:covariant-mellin-class}) with property (1), at every height $c$; in
particular, it is the operator of the signaling problem (2).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(1) The realising function is exhibited: put $h(x) := s\,x\,H(sx)$, so that
$\tilde h(z) = s^{-z}\tilde H(z+1)$, the weight $x$ shifting the Mellin argument by one and the
two powers of $s$ combining as $s\cdot s^{-(z+1)}$. On the other side
$\tilde g(z) := \widetilde{H(s\,\cdot)}(z) = s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$, so
\[
  B(-z)\,\tilde g(z)
  = \frac{\tilde H(z+1)}{\tilde H(z)}\cdot s^{-z}\tilde H(z)
  = s^{-z}\tilde H(z+1)
  = \tilde h(z)
\]
at every point of the line where $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$, which is all but an isolated --- hence
null --- set of it. The cancellation is the whole content, being
Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}'s recursion with the denominator cleared; it should
\emph{not} be described as a product containing no division, since the division is there and
clearing it is what costs the exceptional set. Both convergence hypotheses of
Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator} hold for $h$ at height $c$, being
Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data} and Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-vertical} at height
$c + 1 \in (1, z_*)$, and $h$ is continuous because $H$ is. So $h$ realises
$B(\theta)H(s\,\cdot)$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-operator-action} gives
$A[H(s\cdot)](x) = h(x)/x = s\,H(sx)$: the weight $x^{-1}$ of
Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator} is exactly what cancels the weight $x$ that shifted
the transform, so the exhibition of $h$ and the eigenfunction relation are one statement
rather than two.

(2) Causality and the boundary attainment are (A3) and (A7). The Laplace form is (1) applied
to $\hat u(s, \cdot) = \hat f(s) H(s\,\cdot)$. The Mellin form combines
Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals} with Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol} --- note
that it applies the former at $z - 1$, so it is the wider strip that is used, available
because $f \in \core$ is bounded; and that the cancellation below is of $\tilde H(z-1)$, hence
valid wherever that does not vanish, which by Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol} is all of the
strip off an isolated set:
\[
  B(1-z)\,\widetilde{u(t,\cdot)}(z-1)
  = \frac{\tilde H(z)}{\tilde H(z-1)}\cdot\tilde H(z-1)\,(I^{z-1}f)(t)
  = \tilde H(z)\,(I^{z-1}f)(t)
  = \widetilde{\partial_t u(t,\cdot)}(z).
\]

(3) is Lemma~\ref{lem:symbol-uniqueness}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[classical interpretation; poles]\label{rem:poles}
The time-domain equation in Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) is stated at the level of
Mellin transforms, and that is the honest general formulation: $B$ may have poles, at zeros of
$\tilde H$, and polynomial growth along vertical lines, so $A u(t, \cdot)$ need not be given by
an absolutely convergent integral for rough $f$. On the field the identity is nevertheless
regular, the factor $\tilde H(z-1)$ cancelling every denominator. That last statement is about
the product as a \emph{meromorphic function}; read pointwise it needs $\tilde H(z-1) \ne 0$,
which holds off an isolated set by Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) is stated accordingly. The distinction recurs through this
section --- it is the same one that makes Lemma~\ref{lem:symbol-rigidity}'s conclusion an
equality of meromorphic functions rather than of functions --- and is worth keeping in view: a
cancellation performed on symbols has to be justified at each point where the identity is
used.

When the symbol $B$ is a \emph{polynomial}, $A$ is a local differential operator and the
equation is a classical PDE. That is precisely the situation of Theorem~5$'$ in
\S\ref{sec:locality}, and of Example~\ref{ex:bessel-quadratic} below.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) closes with an informal gloss --- ``that is,
$\partial_t u = A\,u$ read through the Mellin transform in $x$'' --- and clause~(3) proves
uniqueness of the \emph{operator} $A$ within the covariant Mellin class, not uniqueness of the
\emph{solution} $u$: two different claims the single word ``unique'' can hide. The gap is not
merely presentational. The eigenvalue recursion $s\,\tilde g(z) = B(1-z)\,\tilde g(z-1)$ that
pins $\hat u(s,\cdot)$'s Mellin data has the classical period-one ambiguity: its general
solution is $\tilde g(z) = s^{-z}\tilde H(z)\,p(z)$ for an \emph{arbitrary} $1$-periodic $p$,
the same obstruction Bohr--Mollerup closes for $\Gamma$, one level down. What follows closes it
here. Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution} names what a solution of the signaling problem
\emph{is}, mirroring Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2)'s own clauses rather than restating
them; Lemma~\ref{lem:mode-rigidity} kills the periodic ambiguity, under a normalisation the
field satisfies for free --- its own modes are dominated by the profile, uniformly over one
period, the theory's counterpart of the log-convexity hypothesis Bohr--Mollerup imposes; and
Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-wellposed} assembles existence with \emph{solution}-uniqueness
under that normalisation --- existence and uniqueness in the profile-dominated Mellin
class, which Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form} alone does not deliver; of Hadamard's three
demands this supplies the first two, and Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-hadamard} completes
the third from the representation --- with stability under perturbation of the exponent
besides --- so that the problem is well posed in the stated class,
Remark~\ref{rem:signaling-stability} marking the boundary of the claim. A
classical-solution clause, under an extra
decay hypothesis on the negative-moment function $m$ that the Gamma, stable and Bessel-K
families satisfy and the pure delay fails --- correctly, that family belonging instead to
Theorem~\ref{thm:locality}'s territory --- is
Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-classical}.

\begin{definition}[a Mellin solution of the signaling problem]\label{def:mellin-solution}
Assume (H) and work in the canonical gauge, and let $f \in \core$. A \emph{Mellin solution of the
signaling problem for $f$} is a field $u : \RR \times (0,\infty) \to \RR$ such that $u(\cdot, x)
\in \Xz$ for every $x > 0$ --- causal in $t$ at every scale, the honest form of the boundary
condition $u(0,\cdot) = 0$, since $\Xz$ is by definition the causal elements of $X$ --- with
$u(\cdot, x) \to f$ in $\Xz$ as $x \downarrow 0$, and, writing
$\hat u(s,x) := \int_0^\infty e^{-st}u(t,x)\,dt$ for $s > 0$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Laplace form.)} For every $s > 0$: $x \mapsto \hat u(s,x)$ is continuous on
$(0,\infty)$; its Mellin transform $z \mapsto \widetilde{\hat u(s,\cdot)}(z)$ is analytic on the
whole strip $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$; at every height $c \in (0, z_*-1)$, $\hat u(s,\cdot)$ lies in the
domain of $A$ (Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}); and
\[
A\bigl[\hat u(s,\cdot)\bigr] \;=\; s\,\hat u(s,\cdot), \qquad \hat u(s, \zp) \;=\; \hat f(s);
\]
\item \emph{(Mellin form.)} For every $t > 0$ and every $z$ with $1 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ and
$\tilde H(z-1) \ne 0$,
\[
\widetilde{\partial_t u(t, \cdot)}(z) \;=\; B(1-z)\,\widetilde{u(t,\cdot)}(z-1),
\]
both transforms absolutely convergent.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

% shared with blueprint lem:mode-rigidity
\begin{lemma}[rigidity of a profile-dominated mode]\label{lem:mode-rigidity}
Assume (H) and fix $s > 0$. Let $\tilde g$ be analytic on the strip $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ and satisfy
the pole-free eigenvalue recursion
\[
s\,\tilde g(z)\,\tilde H(z-1) \;=\; \tilde H(z)\,\tilde g(z-1), \qquad 1 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*,
\]
an identity of holomorphic functions on that range, with no exceptional set. Suppose the
\emph{periodic factor}
\[
p(z) \;:=\; \frac{s^z\,\tilde g(z)}{\tilde H(z)},
\]
a priori meromorphic on $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ with possible poles only at the isolated zeros of
$\tilde H$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}), is bounded on some period substrip
$\{c \le \operatorname{Re} z \le c+1\} \subset (0, z_*)$, off those zeros. Then $p$ is constant: there is
$\kappa \in \CC$ with
\[
\tilde g(z) \;=\; \kappa\, s^{-z}\,\tilde H(z), \qquad 0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Where $\tilde H(z) \ne 0$ and $\tilde H(z-1) \ne 0$, dividing the recursion by
$s\,\tilde H(z)\tilde H(z-1)$ and multiplying by $s^{z}$ gives $p(z) = p(z-1)$, so the periodic
factor satisfies the shift relation off the (isolated) zeros of $\tilde H$ on both sides.

\textbf{Step 1: $p$ extends to a meromorphic, $1$-periodic function on $\CC$.} $\tilde H$'s zeros
in $(0,z_*)$ are isolated (Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}), hence locally finite there: every
compact subset of the open strip contains finitely many of them --- accumulation at the strip's
\emph{boundary} is not excluded, and is not needed; so, writing
$S := (0,z_*)$ as a set of vertical strips $S_n := S + n = \{z : n < \operatorname{Re} z < n + z_*\}$ for
$n \in \ZZ$, and $p_n(z) := p(z-n)$, meromorphic on $S_n$ with possible poles only at the (locally
finite) translated zeros of $\tilde H$: for $n \le k \le m$ and $z \in S_n \cap S_m$,
$z$ lies in every intermediate $S_k$ (since $\operatorname{Re} z > m \ge k$ and $\operatorname{Re} z < n + z_* \le k + z_*$),
so the shift relation applied at each consecutive pair $(k,k+1)$, $n \le k < m$, gives
$p_n(z) = p_{n+1}(z) = \dots = p_m(z)$: every two translates agree wherever they overlap. Since
$z_* > 1$ (H), consecutive strips overlap and $\{S_n\}_{n \in \ZZ}$ covers $\CC$, so there is a
well-defined meromorphic $P : \CC \to \CC$ with $P|_{S_n} = p_n$ for every $n$; it is $1$-periodic,
since for $z \in S_n$, $z+1 \in S_{n+1}$ and $P(z+1) = p_{n+1}(z+1) = p(z-n) = p_n(z) = P(z)$.

\textbf{Step 2: $P$ is bounded off its singular set, on all of $\CC$.} $P|_{S_0} = p$ is bounded on
the given substrip $\{c \le \operatorname{Re} z \le c+1\}$, off the zeros of $\tilde H$ there, by hypothesis. By
periodicity, $P$ is bounded by the \emph{same} constant on every unit translate
$\{c+n \le \operatorname{Re} z \le c+1+n\}$, $n \in \ZZ$, off $P$'s singular set there; and these translates
cover $\CC$, consecutive ones sharing an edge. So $P$ is bounded, by one constant, off its singular
set, everywhere.

\textbf{Step 3: removability and Liouville.} At each point $w_0$ of $P$'s singular set --- a priori
a pole, though this step shows it is not --- $P$ is bounded on a punctured neighbourhood of $w_0$
(Step 2), so by Riemann's removable singularity theorem $P$ extends to be analytic at $w_0$, with
value the limit from nearby --- hence bounded by the same constant, since nearby values are. Doing
this at every such point is licensed because the singular set is locally finite in $\CC$: every
point of $\CC$ has a compact neighbourhood inside some open $S_n$ --- again $z_* > 1$, the open
intervals $(n, n+z_*)$ covering $\RR$ --- and there the possible poles are the finitely many
translated zeros of Step 1. The result is an \emph{entire} function $\hat P$, equal to $P$ off its
singular set and bounded on all of $\CC$ by the constant of Step 2. Liouville's theorem gives
$\hat P \equiv \kappa$ for some $\kappa \in \CC$.

\textbf{Closing.} On $S_0 = (0,z_*)$, $\hat P = P = p$ off the zeros of $\tilde H$, so
$s^z\tilde g(z) = \kappa\,\tilde H(z)$ for every $z \in (0,z_*)$ off that isolated set. Both sides
are analytic on the whole (connected, open) strip $(0,z_*)$ --- the left because $\tilde g$ is, by
hypothesis; the right because $\tilde H$ is (Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}) --- and agree off a
set with no accumulation point in the strip, so by the identity theorem they agree everywhere on
it. Dividing by $s^z \ne 0$ gives $\tilde g(z) = \kappa\,s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$ for every
$z \in (0,z_*)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[the signaling problem: existence and uniqueness in the profile-dominated Mellin class]\label{cor:signaling-wellposed}
Assume (H), let $f \in \core$, and let $A$ be the inversion operator of
Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Existence.)} The scale-space field $u(t,x) := (\Phi_{0,x}f)(t)$ is a Mellin solution
of the signaling problem for $f$ (Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}); its periodic factor
$p_s(z) := s^z\widetilde{\hat u(s,\cdot)}(z)/\tilde H(z)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:mode-rigidity}) is the
\emph{constant} $\hat f(s)$, for every $s > 0$ --- in particular profile-dominated.
\item \emph{(Uniqueness.)} It is the \emph{unique} Mellin solution of the signaling problem for
$f$ whose modes are profile-dominated --- i.e., such that for every $s > 0$ the periodic factor of
Lemma~\ref{lem:mode-rigidity} is bounded on some period substrip: if $v$ is another such solution,
then $v(\cdot,x) = u(\cdot,x) = (\Phi_{0,x}f)(\cdot)$ in $\Xz$, for every $x > 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(1) is Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(1) for the domain and eigenfunction clauses and
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) for causality, the boundary limit, the Laplace and Mellin
forms; continuity of $x \mapsto \hat u(s,x) = \hat f(s)H(sx)$ and analyticity of its transform on
the whole strip are as in the statement. Since $\widetilde{\hat u(s,\cdot)}(z) =
\hat f(s)\,s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$, the periodic factor is
$p_s(z) = s^z\cdot\hat f(s)\,s^{-z}\tilde H(z)/\tilde H(z) = \hat f(s)$ identically.

(2) Let $v$ be a Mellin solution for $f$ with profile-dominated modes, and fix $s > 0$. Write
$\tilde g(z) := \widetilde{\hat v(s,\cdot)}(z)$, analytic on the whole strip by
Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}(1). At every height $c \in (0,z_*-1)$, $\hat v(s,\cdot)$ lies
in the domain of $A$ with $A[\hat v(s,\cdot)] = s\,\hat v(s,\cdot)$; taking Mellin transforms
(Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}'s transform identity, as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(1)) gives $s\,\tilde g(z) = B(1-z)\tilde g(z-1)$ off the zeros of
$\tilde H(z-1)$ on the line $\operatorname{Re} z = c+1$; multiplying out
$B(1-z) = \tilde H(z)/\tilde H(z-1)$ clears the denominator to
$s\,\tilde g(z)\tilde H(z-1) = \tilde H(z)\tilde g(z-1)$ off an isolated set of the line, and both
sides are analytic on the strip $1 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$ (the left by hypothesis, the right by
Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}), so by the identity theorem the identity holds \emph{everywhere}
on that range --- the pole-free recursion Lemma~\ref{lem:mode-rigidity} asks for. By hypothesis
its periodic factor is bounded on some period substrip, so Lemma~\ref{lem:mode-rigidity} gives
$\kappa_s \in \CC$ with $\tilde g(z) = \kappa_s\,s^{-z}\tilde H(z)$ for every $z \in (0,z_*)$ ---
that is, $\widetilde{\hat v(s,\cdot)}$ and $\widetilde{\kappa_s H(s\cdot)}$ agree on the strip.

Both $x \mapsto \hat v(s,x)$ (continuous, Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}(1)) and
$x \mapsto \kappa_s H(sx)$ (continuous, $H$ a Laplace transform) have this same Mellin transform,
absolutely convergent on the line $\operatorname{Re} z = c+1$ for any $c \in (0,z_*-1)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-vertical}, up to the constant $\kappa_s$). Mellin inversion on a line of
absolute convergence recovers a continuous function from its transform, so
\[
\hat v(s,x) \;=\; \kappa_s\,H(sx), \qquad x > 0.
\]
Letting $x \downarrow 0$ on both (continuous) sides and using $\hat v(s,\zp) = \hat f(s)$
(Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}(1)) and $H(0) = 1$ ($H$ the Laplace transform of the law of
$T_1$) gives $\kappa_s = \hat f(s)$. So $\hat v(s,x) = \hat f(s)H(sx) = \hat u(s,x)$ for every
$x>0$ and every $s>0$: $t \mapsto v(t,x)$ and $t \mapsto u(t,x)$, both in $\Xz$
(Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}(1) for $v$, Lemma~\ref{lem:delay-core} for
$u = \Phi_{0,x}f \in \core \subset \Xz$), have the same Laplace transform for every $s>0$, so by
Corollary~\ref{cor:laplace-uniqueness-signed} % blueprint cites prop:laplace-uniqueness-causal; the signed reduction is paper-only
applied to their difference
they agree: $v(\cdot,x) = u(\cdot,x)$ in $\Xz$, for every $x>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[classical solutions of the signaling problem]\label{cor:signaling-classical}
Assume (H), let $f \in \core$ with $f'$ additionally bounded and continuous, and let
$u(t,x) := (\Phi_{0,x}f)(t)$ be the scale-space field. Suppose that for some $c \in (0,z_*-1)$,
\[
\int_{\RR} (1+|\tau|)\,\bigl|m(c+1+i\tau)\bigr|\,d\tau \;<\; \infty, \qquad m(z) := \Ex\bigl[T_1^{-z}\bigr]
\]
--- decay of the negative-moment function along one vertical line. Then for every $t > 0$, the
function $x \mapsto u(t,x)$ lies in the domain of $A$ at height $c$, with realising function
$h(x) = x\,\partial_t u(t,x)$, and
\[
\partial_t u(t,x) \;=\; \bigl(A\,u(t,\cdot)\bigr)(x) \qquad \text{at every } x > 0:
\]
the field solves the signaling equation \emph{classically}, pointwise in $x$ and at every $t$, not
merely at the level of Mellin transforms.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals}, $u(\cdot,x)$ is the primitive of the field of
$f'$: $u(t,x) = \int_0^t \Ex[f'(\rho - xT_1)]\,d\rho$ for every $t$. The integrand
$\rho \mapsto \Ex[f'(\rho-xT_1)]$ is continuous, by dominated convergence ($f'$ bounded and
continuous, $|f'| \le \|f'\|_\infty$ an integrable dominating constant on any compact
$\rho$-range), so the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $t \mapsto u(t,x)$ a genuine pointwise
derivative at \emph{every} $t$, equal to $g(x) := \Ex[f'(t-xT_1)]$ --- continuous in $x$ by the
same dominated convergence argument, run in $x$ instead of $\rho$. Set
$h(x) := x\,g(x) = x\,\partial_t u(t,x)$, continuous.

$\tilde H(z)(I^{z-1}f)(t) = \widetilde{\partial_t u(t,\cdot)}(z)$ for $0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:memory-fractional-integrals}, the derivative clause, extended strip since
$f \in \core$ is bounded), and the weight $x$ shifts the Mellin argument by one, so
$\tilde h(z) = \widetilde{\partial_t u(t,\cdot)}(z+1)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-symbol}'s
recursion $B(-z) = \tilde H(z+1)/\tilde H(z)$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2)'s transform
identity shifted by one, $\tilde h(z) = B(-z)\,\widetilde{u(t,\cdot)}(z)$ for
$0 < \operatorname{Re} z < z_*-1$, off the isolated zeros of $\tilde H(z)$: the realising-function condition
of Definition~\ref{def:inversion-operator}, with $u(t,\cdot)$ in the role of its $g$, at symbol
$B$, height $c$.

For the vertical-integrability bound, write $z = c+1+i\tau$. By definition of $I$,
$\Gamma(z-1)(I^{z-1}f)(t) = \int_0^t (t-r)^{z-2}f(r)\,dr$, and $f \in \core$ is bounded
($\|f\|_\infty \le \|f'\|_1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:delay-core}), so
\[
\bigl|\Gamma(z-1)(I^{z-1}f)(t)\bigr| \;\le\; \|f\|_\infty \int_0^t (t-r)^{\operatorname{Re} z - 2}\,dr
\;=\; \|f\|_\infty\,\frac{t^{\operatorname{Re} z - 1}}{\operatorname{Re} z - 1} \;=\; \|f\|_\infty\,\frac{t^{c}}{c}
\]
(using $\operatorname{Re} z = c+1 > 1$). Since $\Gamma(z) = (z-1)\Gamma(z-1)$,
$|\Gamma(z)(I^{z-1}f)(t)| \le |z-1|\,\|f\|_\infty\,t^c/c$, and
$\widetilde{\partial_t u(t,\cdot)}(z) = m(z)\,\Gamma(z)(I^{z-1}f)(t)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-data}), so
\[
\bigl|\tilde h(c+i\tau)\bigr| \;=\; \bigl|\widetilde{\partial_t u(t,\cdot)}(c+1+i\tau)\bigr|
\;\le\; \frac{\|f\|_\infty\,t^c}{c}\;|c+i\tau|\;\bigl|m(c+1+i\tau)\bigr|.
\]
Since $|c+i\tau| \le (c+1)(1+|\tau|)$, the displayed hypothesis makes the right side integrable in
$\tau$ over $\RR$: $\tilde h$ is vertically integrable at height $c$.

So $u(t,\cdot)$ lies in the domain of $A$ at height $c$ with realising function $h$, which is
continuous; Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion-operator-action} gives
$A[u(t,\cdot)](x) = h(x)/x = g(x) = \partial_t u(t,x)$, at every $x > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[which families are classical]\label{rem:classical-families}
Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-classical}'s hypothesis is a statement about
$m(z) = \Ex[T_1^{-z}]$'s decay along one vertical line, checked against every family the article
names.

\textbf{Gamma} ($F(s) = \gamma\log(1+s)$, $\gamma>1$, Example~\ref{ex:gamma-signaling}):
$m(z) = \Gamma(\gamma-z)/\Gamma(\gamma)$, analytic for $\operatorname{Re} z < \gamma$. For
$c \in (0,\gamma-1)$, $\gamma - (c+1) \in (0,\gamma-1)$ avoids $\Gamma$'s poles, and the classical
vertical-line decay $|\Gamma(\sigma+i\tau)| \sim \sqrt{2\pi}\,|\tau|^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi|\tau|/2}$
(already used at Lemma~\ref{lem:mellin-vertical}) gives $|m(c+1+i\tau)|$ exponential decay in
$|\tau|$: the hypothesis holds.

\textbf{Stable}, $\alpha<1$ ($F(s)=s^\alpha$, Example~\ref{ex:stable-signaling}):
$m(z) = \Gamma(z/\alpha)/(\alpha\Gamma(z))$. The same asymptotic applied to numerator and
denominator gives $|m(c+1+i\tau)|$ decaying like $e^{-(\pi/2)(1/\alpha-1)|\tau|}$ up to a
polynomial factor, exponential since $\alpha<1$: the hypothesis holds.

\textbf{Bessel-K} ($T_1 = 1/(2\gamma_a)$, Proposition~\ref{prop:bessel-family};
$m(z) = 2^z\Gamma(a+z)/\Gamma(a)$, Example~\ref{ex:bessel-quadratic}):
$|2^{c+1+i\tau}| = 2^{c+1}$ is constant in $\tau$, so the decay of $|m(c+1+i\tau)|$ is exactly
that of $|\Gamma(a+c+1+i\tau)|$ --- exponential, by the same asymptotic as the Gamma family.
\textbf{Checked directly, not assumed}: the closed form $2^z\Gamma(a+z)$ looks, read along the
real axis, like it grows without bound, but $2^{i\tau}$ has modulus $1$ and contributes nothing to
the decay along a \emph{vertical} line; the family is classical.

\textbf{Pure delay} ($F(s)=b_0s$, $b_0>0$): $T_1 \equiv b_0$ a.s., so $m(z) = b_0^{-z}$, and
$|m(c+1+i\tau)| = b_0^{-(c+1)}$ is constant in $\tau$: not integrable against $(1+|\tau|)\,d\tau$.
The hypothesis fails, correctly. The transport operator's eigenfunctions are the constant-modulus
exponentials, whose Mellin data does not decay, and this family's inversion is instead
Theorem~\ref{thm:locality}'s territory --- a polynomial (here, first-order) symbol, local by
construction, not reached by Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-classical}'s route at all.
\end{remark}

What remains of Hadamard's demands is continuous dependence, and it costs nothing: the
representation supplies it, together with a stability under perturbation of the exponent
itself that the equation alone would not suggest. The assembly earns the classical phrase.

\begin{corollary}[the signaling problem is well posed]\label{cor:signaling-hadamard}
Assume (H), work in the canonical gauge, and let $f \in \core$, with
$u(t,x) := (\Phi_{0,x}f)(t)$ the scale-space field. Then the signaling problem for $f$
satisfies all three of Hadamard's demands --- existence, uniqueness, continuous dependence
on the data --- and is stable under perturbation of the exponent besides:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Existence.)} $u$ is a Mellin solution of the signaling problem for $f$
(Definition~\ref{def:mellin-solution}), with profile-dominated modes (Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-wellposed}(1)).
\item \emph{(Uniqueness.)} It is the unique Mellin solution for $f$ with profile-dominated
modes (Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-wellposed}(2)).
\item \emph{(Continuous dependence on the data.)} For $g \in \core$ with field
$v(t,x) := (\Phi_{0,x}g)(t)$,
\[
  \sup_{x>0}\,\|u(\cdot,x) - v(\cdot,x)\|_1 \;\le\; \|f - g\|_1,
  \qquad
  \|u(\cdot,y) - v(\cdot,y)\|_1 \;\le\; \|u(\cdot,x) - v(\cdot,x)\|_1 \quad (0 < x < y).
\]
\item \emph{(Stability under perturbation of the exponent.)} Let $F_n$ be admissible
exponents with $F_n(s) \to F(s)$ for every $s > 0$, and let
$u_n(t,x) := (\mu^{(n)}_{0,x} * f)(t)$ be the corresponding fields, $\mu^{(n)}_{0,x}$ the
canonical-gauge kernel of $F_n$ --- the law of $x\,T^{(n)}_1$ with
$\Ex[e^{-sT^{(n)}_1}] = e^{-F_n(s)}$. Then for every $x > 0$,
\[
  u_n(\cdot, x) \;\to\; u(\cdot, x) \quad\text{in } \Xz .
\]
When every $F_n$ satisfies (H), the left side is, by (1)--(2) applied to $F_n$, the unique
profile-dominated Mellin solution of the perturbed problem: the solutions of the perturbed
problems converge, at every scale, to the solution of the limit problem.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(1) and (2) are Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-wellposed}, restated clause for clause.

(3) By Theorem~\ref{thm:main-characterization} the solution operator at scale $x$ is convolution
with the probability measure $\mu_{0,x}$, so $u(\cdot,x) - v(\cdot,x) = \mu_{0,x}*(f-g)$;
and convolution with a probability measure $\mu$ carried by $[0,\infty)$ is nonexpansive on
$X$: by Tonelli,
$\int |(\mu * h)(t)|\,dt \le \int\!\!\int |h(t-y)|\,d\mu(y)\,dt = \mu([0,\infty))\,\|h\|_1
= \|h\|_1$. That gives the first display, uniformly in $x$. For $0 < x < y$ the hemigroup
factorizes $\Phi_{0,y} = \Phi_{x,y}\Phi_{0,x}$, so
$u(\cdot,y) - v(\cdot,y) = \mu_{x,y} * \bigl(u(\cdot,x) - v(\cdot,x)\bigr)$ with $\mu_{x,y}$
again a probability measure, and the same bound applies.

(4) Two steps: the kernels converge weakly, and weak convergence convolves into
$\Xz$-convergence.

\emph{The kernels.} Fix $x > 0$. The Laplace transform of $\mu^{(n)}_{0,x}$ at $s$ is
$e^{-F_n(xs)}$, which converges to $e^{-F(xs)}$, the transform of $\mu_{0,x}$, for every
$s > 0$; so $\mu^{(n)}_{0,x} \to \mu_{0,x}$ weakly by Proposition~\ref{prop:laplace-continuity}. (The
same argument runs on any increment: the transform of $\mu^{(n)}_{x,y}$ is
$e^{-(F_n(ys) - F_n(xs))}$, so every $\mu^{(n)}_{x,y} \to \mu_{x,y}$ weakly as well.)

\emph{The fields.} We show that $\mu_n \to \mu$ weakly, for probability measures on
$[0,\infty)$, forces $\mu_n * h \to \mu * h$ in $X$ for every $h \in X$, and apply this at
$\mu_n = \mu^{(n)}_{0,x}$, $h = f$. First let $g$ be continuous with compact support and
$g \ge 0$. Then $(\mu_n * g)(t) = \int g(t-y)\,d\mu_n(y) \to (\mu * g)(t)$ at every $t$,
the integrand being bounded and continuous in $y$; and the integrals are all equal: by
Tonelli, $\int (\mu_n * g) = \mu_n([0,\infty))\int g = \int g$, and likewise for $\mu$.
Pointwise convergence of nonnegative integrable functions whose integrals converge to the
limit's integral upgrades to $L^1$ convergence --- Scheffé's lemma, and here it is one
line: writing $a_+ := \max(a, 0)$,
\[
  \int\bigl|\mu_n * g - \mu * g\bigr|
  \;=\; \int(\mu_n * g - \mu * g) \;+\; 2\int\bigl(\mu * g - \mu_n * g\bigr)_+ ,
\]
the first term zero as just computed, and the second tending to zero by dominated
convergence, since $0 \le (\mu * g - \mu_n * g)_+ \le \mu * g \in L^1$ and the integrand
tends to zero pointwise. A general continuous compactly supported $g$ splits as
$g_+ - g_-$, both again continuous with compact support. Finally, for $h \in X$ and
$\varepsilon > 0$ pick such a $g$ with $\|h - g\|_1 < \varepsilon$ --- continuous functions
of compact support are dense in $L^1$ --- and the contraction of (3) closes the estimate:
\[
  \|\mu_n * h - \mu * h\|_1
  \;\le\; \|\mu_n * (h - g)\|_1 + \|\mu_n * g - \mu * g\|_1 + \|\mu * (g - h)\|_1
  \;\le\; 2\varepsilon + \|\mu_n * g - \mu * g\|_1 ,
\]
whose limit superior over $n$ is at most $2\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon > 0$.
Applied at $\mu_n = \mu^{(n)}_{0,x}$ and $h = f$ this gives $u_n(\cdot,x) \to u(\cdot,x)$
in $X$; every field here is causal, $f$ and the kernels being carried by $[0,\infty)$, so
the convergence is in $\Xz$. The closing sentence of (4) is (1)--(2) applied to $F_n$,
whose hypotheses --- (H) for the exponent, $f \in \core$ for the data --- are exactly what
is assumed.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the stability rests on; what is not claimed]\label{rem:signaling-stability}
The stability clauses of Corollary~\ref{cor:signaling-hadamard} come from the representation rather
than from the equation: the solution operator at each scale is convolution with a
probability measure (Theorem~\ref{thm:main-characterization}), and the exponent stability is the
continuity theorem for Laplace transforms (Proposition~\ref{prop:laplace-continuity}) read on the
kernels. Neither uses (H), which enters only through the solution concept of clauses
(1)--(2); both therefore hold for every admissible family and every signal in $X$, not
only on the class the signaling problem is posed on. In the scale-Cauchy reading of
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-memory}, the second contraction of clause~(3) says that a perturbation of
the record at one scale does not grow at any coarser one. What is \emph{not} claimed is a
stability estimate in a topology adapted to $A$ itself --- continuity of the solution in
the Mellin-class data, or convergence of the operators behind clause~(4): the conclusion
there is convergence of the solution fields, never of the symbols, and the distinction is
real --- $F(s) + s/n \to F(s)$ pointwise with every perturbed exponent carrying drift, so
$z_* = \infty$ along the whole sequence (Lemma~\ref{lem:zstar-log-growth}(2)) while the limit's
$z_*$ may be finite, and the strips the symbols live on do not converge to the limit's.
Whether the signaling problem is well posed in a topology adapted to $A$ is a question
this article leaves open.
\end{remark}

Three worked cases calibrate the construction, ordered by the growth of the symbol ---
bounded, quadratic, fractional --- which is the order of the media's regularity: an
integral operator, a classical PDE, and a fractional operator whose medium, for
$\alpha < \tfrac12$, does not exist at all.

\begin{example}[the Gamma family; scale-recursive form]\label{ex:gamma-signaling}
For $F(s) = \gamma \log(1+s)$ with $\gamma > 1$ we have
$\tilde H(z) = \Gamma(z)\Gamma(\gamma - z)/\Gamma(\gamma)$ and $B(-z) = z/(\gamma - 1 - z)$,
i.e.
\[
  B(\theta) = -\,\theta\,\bigl(\theta + \gamma - 1\bigr)^{-1},
  \qquad A = -\,\frac{1}{x}\,\theta\,\bigl(\theta + \gamma - 1\bigr)^{-1},
\]
a \emph{rational} Mellin symbol --- the memory-line mirror of the time-side resolvent
$\gamma\,\partial_t(1 + x\partial_t)^{-1}$ of Example~\ref{ex:gamma-recursive} in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-memory}. Explicitly,
$w := (\theta + \gamma - 1)^{-1} u$ solves $x\,w_x + (\gamma-1)w = u$, with the regular-at-$0$
solution
\[
  w(t, x) = x^{-(\gamma-1)}\int_0^x y^{\gamma - 2}\, u(t, y)\,dy,
\]
so the time evolution at scale $x$ is computed from the field at \emph{finer} scales by a
single weighted average --- scale-recursive, one-sided from the boundary, matching the causal
architecture of the time side.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[parabolic gauge; the Bessel family is the quadratic case]\label{ex:bessel-quadratic}
In the $\alpha$-gauge (similarity variable $x^{1/\alpha} s$; Remark~\ref{rem:gauge-freedom})
the profiles are $H(x^{1/\alpha}s)$, the Euler operator acts on the profile through
$\alpha^{-1}\theta$, and the inversion becomes $A = x^{-1/\alpha}\,B(\alpha\,\theta)$. Take the
parabolic gauge $\alpha = \tfrac12$, i.e.\ the coordinate $\xi$ of Remark~\ref{rem:gauge-freedom},
and the Bessel-K family of Proposition~\ref{prop:bessel-family}, $T_1 = 1/(2\gamma_a)$: then
$\Ex[T_1^{-z}] = 2^z\,\Gamma(a + z)/\Gamma(a)$, so
\[
  B(-z) = 2z(z + a), \qquad
  A = \xi^{-2}\Bigl(\tfrac{\theta^2}{2} - a\theta\Bigr)
  = \tfrac12\,\partial_\xi^2 + \frac{\beta}{\xi}\,\partial_\xi, \quad \beta = \tfrac12 - a .
\]
The symbol is a \emph{quadratic polynomial}, so $A$ is local: the signaling form of the
Bessel-K scale space is exactly the Bessel diffusion-with-drift medium, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}(2) becomes the classical signaling problem
$\partial_t u = \tfrac12 u_{\xi\xi} + \tfrac{\beta}{\xi}u_\xi$ with $u(t,0) = f(t)$ and
$u(0,\xi) = 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[extremal stable; recovering Theorem 4 of 2005]\label{ex:stable-signaling}
For $F(s) = s^\alpha$ we have $\tilde H(z) = \Gamma(z/\alpha)/\alpha$ and
\[
  B(-z) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac{z+1}{\alpha}\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac{z}{\alpha}\bigr)}
  \;\sim\; \Bigl(\tfrac{z}{\alpha}\Bigr)^{1/\alpha} \quad (z \to \infty),
\]
a symbol of order $1/\alpha$. On the profiles $e^{-(sx)^\alpha}$ the right-sided Liouville
derivative acts by $D^{1/\alpha}_{x,-} e^{-cx} = c^{1/\alpha} e^{-cx}$ with $c = s^\alpha$,
i.e.\ it also produces the eigenvalue $s$; so the Mellin representative and the operator of
\sscite{fagerstrom2005temporal}, Thm.~4, agree on the span of the field profiles. The
translation-invariant representative is a coincidence of homogeneity; the Mellin
representative is the one that survives in general.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Markov media]\label{rem:markov-media}
Within the class of Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form}, the operators $A$ that additionally
satisfy the positive maximum principle (Definition~\ref{def:locality-pmp}) --- i.e.\ generate a
sub-Markov process on the memory half-line, so that the medium is a physical transport medium
--- are --- we record this without proof here --- after the logarithmic substitution
$x = e^\xi$, exactly those whose symbol $B$ is the Laplace exponent of a Lévy process. By the
Lamperti correspondence (\sscite{lamperti1972semi}) these media are positive
self-similar Markov processes absorbed at the boundary, and the kernels are the
boundary-arrival times $T_x \stackrel{d}{=}$ absorption time from depth $x$.

The spectral orientation of the jumps is \emph{not} constrained. For spectrally positive media
the process creeps downward through every level and the hemigroup (A6) holds \emph{pathwise},
the kernels being genuine level-passage times; but media with downward jumps are also
admissible, the factorization then holding in law though not pathwise, being inherited from
the exponent $F$ rather than from the paths.

The Gamma family is the standard example: unfolding Example~\ref{ex:gamma-signaling}, its
medium is the multiplicative jump process that waits at scale $x$ an
$\mathrm{Exp}(\text{mean } x)$ time and jumps to $\sigma x$ with
$\sigma \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\gamma-1, 1)$, reaching the boundary by accumulation of jumps; a
first-jump computation confirms $\Ex_x[e^{-s\tau_0}] = (1+sx)^{-\gamma}$.

A necessary condition in all cases --- again recorded without proof --- is quadratic symbol
growth, $B(\theta) = O(\theta^2)$; by Example~\ref{ex:stable-signaling} the homogeneous members
with $\alpha < \tfrac12$, of symbol order $1/\alpha > 2$, admit \emph{no} Markov medium, although
Theorem~\ref{thm:signaling-form} always supplies the analytic signaling form --- the operator
exists, the process does not. The locality theorem settles the local sub-case --- a polynomial
symbol of degree at most two --- but the general classification of which admissible $F$ are
Markov-realizable is the memory-line inverse problem, and it is left open; it is listed with the
open problems in \S\ref{sec:conclusions}.
\end{remark}
