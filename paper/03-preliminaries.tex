\section{Preliminaries and notation}\label{sec:preliminaries}

\emph{Spaces.} Time runs over $\RR$. We write $X = \Lone$, real, with positive cone $X_+$
and $\int f := \int_{\RR} f(t)\,dt$; its dual is $X^* = L^\infty(\RR)$, with pairing
$\langle g, f\rangle = \int g f$. We write $\Mplus(\RR)$ for the finite positive Borel
measures on $\RR$, with total mass $\|\mu\| = \mu(\RR)$. For a function $g$ on $(0,\infty)$,
$g(\zp)$ denotes its limit from the right at $0$; and $\Distr(\RR)$ denotes the space of
distributions, the dual of $C_c^\infty(\RR)$, used only in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-memory}.

\emph{Actions} (as in \S\ref{sec:measurement-covariance}). Translations are
$(\shift_a f)(t) = f(t-a)$, $a \in \RR$. Dilations act on
functions by $(\dil_\sigma f)(t) = \sigma^{-1} f(t/\sigma)$, $\sigma > 0$, which preserves
mass, and on measures by $\dil_\sigma \mu :=$ the pushforward of $\mu$ under
$t \mapsto \sigma t$.

\emph{Convolution.} $(\mu * f)(t) = \int f(t - r)\,\mu(dr)$ for $\mu \in \Mplus(\RR)$,
$f \in X$. Then $\|\mu * f\|_1 \le \|\mu\|\,\|f\|_1$, and dilation is compatible with
convolution:
\[
  \dil_\sigma(\mu * f) = (\dil_\sigma \mu) * (\dil_\sigma f).
\]

\begin{remark}[the signal model; bounded signals]\label{rem:signal-model}
The working space is $X = \Lone$: it is where the Laplace transforms that carry the
characterization live, and every statement of this paper is proved there. The smoothing
itself asks for less. The kernels will turn out to be \emph{probability} measures, and for
$\mu$ a probability measure and $f$ bounded and measurable the same formula defines the
field pointwise,
\[
  (\mu * f)(t) \;=\; \Ex\bigl[f(t - T)\bigr], \qquad T \sim \mu,
\]
with $\|\mu * f\|_\infty \le \|f\|_\infty$, and the cascade law survives by Fubini. What
genuinely needs $X$ is the analysis --- the transforms, hence the characterization theorem
and everything derived through it. What survives the extension unchanged is the
measurement reading: the pointwise field, positivity and the sup bound, the sign-change
statements of Proposition~\ref{prop:gamma-regularity} (stated there for integrable
\emph{or bounded} arguments), and, in the experiment of \S\ref{sec:jet}, the persistent
step --- bounded, not integrable. We record the extension without developing it
systematically.
\end{remark}

\emph{Transforms.} For $\mu \in \Mplus$ supported on $[0,\infty)$ the Laplace transform is
\[
  \hat\mu(s) = \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-st}\,\mu(dt), \qquad s \ge 0 ,
\]
and $\hat\mu(s) > 0$ for all $s \ge 0$ whenever $\mu \ne 0$, the integrand being positive.

Two classes of functions on $(0,\infty)$ carry the analysis: the completely monotone
functions, which will be the transforms of the kernels, and the Bernstein functions, which
will be their exponents.

% shared with blueprint def:completely-monotone
\begin{definition}[completely monotone]\label{def:completely-monotone}
A function $f : (0,\infty) \to \RR$ is \emph{completely monotone} (CM) if $f \in C^\infty$
and $(-1)^n f^{(n)} \ge 0$ for all $n \ge 0$.
\end{definition}

% shared with blueprint def:bernstein-function
\begin{definition}[Bernstein function]\label{def:bernstein-function}
A function $g : (0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)$ is a \emph{Bernstein function} if $g \in C^\infty$
and $g'$ is completely monotone. We write $g \in \BF$, and $g \in \BFz$ if moreover
$g(\zp) = 0$.
\end{definition}

The toolbox that connects the two classes to Laplace transforms is classical, and it is
cited rather than reproven: part~(1) is \sscite{feller2009introduction}, Vol.~2,
\S~XIII.4, Theorems~1 and~1a, pp.~439--440; parts~(2)--(4) are
\sscite{schilling2012bernstein}, Theorem~3.7, p.~27, Theorem~3.2, pp.~21--22, and
Corollary~3.8, pp.~28--29, respectively.

% shared with blueprint prop:bernstein-toolbox
\begin{proposition}[the Bernstein-function toolbox]\label{prop:bernstein-toolbox}
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Bernstein--Widder / Feller criterion.) $f$ is the Laplace transform of a probability
measure on $[0,\infty)$ if and only if $f$ is completely monotone and $f(\zp) = 1$. More
generally, $f$ is completely monotone if and only if $f(s) = \int_{[0,\infty)} e^{-st}\,F(dt)$
for a measure $F$ on $[0,\infty)$, not necessarily finite, and $F$ is then unique.
\item For $g : (0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)$ the following are equivalent: $g \in \BF$; the
composition $h \circ g$ is completely monotone for every completely monotone $h$; and
$e^{-\tau g}$ is completely monotone for every $\tau > 0$.
\item Every $g \in \BF$ is nonnegative, nondecreasing and concave, and has the
Lévy--Khintchine representation
\[
  g(s) = a + b s + \int_0^\infty \bigl(1 - e^{-st}\bigr)\,\nu(dt), \qquad
  a, b \ge 0, \quad \int_0^\infty (1 \wedge t)\,\nu(dt) < \infty,
\]
with $g(\zp) = a$. The triple $(a, b, \nu)$ is unique.
\item $\BF$ is a convex cone, closed under pointwise limits: if $g_n \in \BF$ and
$g_n(s) \to g(s) < \infty$ for every $s > 0$, then $g \in \BF$. Consequently $\BF$ is closed
under mixtures $s \mapsto \int g_u(s)\,m(du)$ with $m \ge 0$, whenever the integral is finite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Which vocabulary does the work.} Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox} is used as a
bridge and not as a toolkit. The object the arguments below manipulate is the
\emph{representation}, part~(3) --- a drift and a Lévy measure --- and almost every proof in
\S\S\ref{sec:representation}--\ref{sec:signaling} works with it directly. The increments of
Theorem~\ref{thm:increments-bernstein} are produced as representations, not as pointwise
limits of Bernstein functions; the potential kernel of the appendix is constructed as a
subordinator's potential measure, not represented from complete monotonicity; the Mellin
calculus of \S\ref{sec:signaling} mentions neither class. Complete monotonicity and the
derivative-sign definition of $\BF$ enter where the $\CM$ class is itself the subject
(Proposition~\ref{prop:pair-regularity}(2), Proposition~\ref{prop:thorin-subclass}), in
Lemma~\ref{lem:selfdecomposable-exponents}'s implications
(1)~$\Rightarrow$~(2)~$\Rightarrow$~(3), and in the classical proofs of \S\ref{sec:characterization},
where the printed route and the machine-checked route differ
(\S\ref{sec:machine-checked}); Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox} identifies the two
vocabularies once so that no later proof has to.

The same class carries a second name, after its representation rather than its derivative
signs; several statements below are given in both forms, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(3) is the identification.

% shared with blueprint def:levy-exponent
\begin{definition}[Lévy exponent]\label{def:levy-exponent}
A function $g : [0,\infty) \to [0,\infty]$ is a \emph{Lévy exponent} if
\[
  g(s) = b_0\,s + \int_0^\infty \bigl(1 - e^{-st}\bigr)\,\nu(dt),
  \qquad b_0 \ge 0,
\]
for a drift $b_0$ and a positive Borel measure $\nu$ on $(0,\infty)$ --- with no killing term,
so that $g(0) = 0$. We write $g \in \LE$, and call $(b_0, \nu)$ its \emph{triple}.
\end{definition}

The definition is valued in $[0,\infty]$ so that it carries no integrability hypothesis;
for a measure $\nu$ on $(0,\infty)$, finiteness of $g$ at a single $s > 0$ is equivalent to
$\int_0^\infty (1 \wedge t)\,\nu(dt) < \infty$. On the finite members, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(3), $\LE = \BFz$ as classes of functions, with the
triple unique. Both names are kept because the two readings matter separately: the proofs
of this paper argue mostly in the representation, and the machine-checked development
(\S\ref{sec:machine-checked}) argues \emph{only} there --- it never defines complete
monotonicity at all --- so a statement whose conclusion is given in $\LE$ is one the
formalization can state verbatim.

\begin{remark}[the circle of ideas]\label{rem:circle-of-ideas}
The classes just named are one circle of ideas, and the reader who sees the circle once
does not need to memorize the toolbox. Start with complete monotonicity. The prototype
completely monotone function is a single decaying exponential $e^{-st}$, whose
derivatives alternate for the trivial reason that each differentiation pulls down a
factor $-t \le 0$. The sign conditions are linear in $f$ and survive pointwise limits,
so every positive mixture $\int e^{-st}\,F(dt)$ --- every Laplace transform of a
positive measure --- is completely monotone for free. The Bernstein--Widder theorem,
Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(1), is the remarkable converse: \emph{every}
completely monotone function is such a mixture, for a unique $F$. Geometrically: $\CM$
is the closed convex cone whose extreme rays are the exponentials, and the countably
many derivative-sign conditions are exactly the visible shadow of positivity of the
mixing measure. Complete monotonicity is to the Laplace transform what positive
definiteness is to the Fourier transform --- the intrinsic, transform-free description
of ``comes from a positive mass.''

Bernstein functions are the same picture one level up, prompted by the question the
cascade forces: for which $g \ge 0$ does $e^{-\tau g}$ remain a Laplace transform for
\emph{every} $\tau > 0$ --- every $\tau$, because a cascade subdivides its exponents
without limit (\S\ref{sec:cascade})? The answer is
Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(2): exactly the Bernstein functions. And the
cone $\BF$ has its own extreme rays, mirroring $\CM$'s exponentials: the elementary
exponents $1 - e^{-st}$, the drift $s$, and the constants. That is what the
Lévy--Khintchine representation, Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(3),
\emph{says} --- the same cone described twice, once by sign conditions (checkable on a
given function) and once by its extreme rays (structural). Definition~\ref{def:levy-exponent}
above chooses the extreme-ray description as primary, which is also the machine-checked
development's choice.

Probability owns this cone through infinite divisibility. A law $\mu$ on $[0,\infty)$
divides, $\mu = \mu_n^{*n}$ for every $n$, exactly when $\hat\mu^{1/n}$ is again a
transform for every $n$ --- that is, when $\hat\mu = e^{-g}$ with $e^{-g/n}$ completely
monotone for all $n$, i.e.\ $g \in \BFz$. And each extreme ray then acquires a
probabilistic identity. Let delay events of size $t$ occur at rate $\lambda$,
independently across disjoint stretches of an interval of length $\tau$: one
infinitesimal slot contributes the transform factor
$\bigl(1 - \lambda\,d\tau\bigr) + \lambda\,d\tau\,e^{-st}
 = 1 - \lambda\,d\tau\,(1 - e^{-st})$,
and compounding the independent slots gives
\[
  \Ex\, e^{-sX_\tau} \;=\; \exp\bigl[-\lambda\tau\,(1 - e^{-st})\bigr] :
\]
the extreme ray $1 - e^{-st}$ is the exponent of a Poisson stream of size-$t$ jumps,
and the Lévy--Khintchine integral is a superposition of independent such streams, one
per jump size --- with the drift as the deterministic limit and the killing constant
$a$, excluded from $\LE$ by $g(0) = 0$, as loss of mass. Spread the divisions out as a
flow and this is a \emph{subordinator}: an increasing process with stationary
independent increments and $\Ex\,e^{-sX_\tau} = e^{-\tau g(s)}$, whose triple describes
the path --- speed $b_0$, jumps of size in $dt$ at rate $\nu(dt)$, the integrability
$\int (1 \wedge t)\,\nu(dt) < \infty$ being exactly path-finiteness. The hemigroup of
this paper relaxes precisely the stationarity: its exponent is $G(x,s)$ rather than
$\tau g(s)$, its increments are independent but not stationary, and the classes
$\SDclass \supset \GGC$ below are the strata of that relaxation under covariance.

The physical reading of the triple --- drift as latency, $\nu$ as a catalogue of
delays, the missing Gaussian term --- is Remark~\ref{rem:delay-dictionary} below. The
extreme-ray pattern returns twice more, with the atoms reshaped by each added
constraint: the Dickman exponents as the extreme rays of the self-decomposable cone
(Proposition~\ref{prop:extreme-rays}), and the Gamma exponents as the atoms of its
Thorin subcone (Proposition~\ref{prop:thorin-subclass}).
\end{remark}

% shared with blueprint lem:vanishing
\begin{lemma}[vanishing lemma]\label{lem:vanishing}
If $g \in \BFz$ and $g(s_0) = 0$ for some $s_0 > 0$, then $g \equiv 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$g$ is nonnegative and concave by Proposition~\ref{prop:bernstein-toolbox}(3), with
$g(\zp) = g(s_0) = 0$. A nonnegative concave function vanishing at both ends of $[0, s_0]$
vanishes on it, so $g \equiv 0$ on $(0, s_0]$ and hence $g' \equiv 0$ on $(0, s_0)$. Now $g'$
is nonincreasing, being the derivative of a concave function, and nonnegative, being
completely monotone; a nonnegative nonincreasing function that vanishes on $(0,s_0)$ vanishes
identically. So $g' \equiv 0$ on $(0,\infty)$, and with $g(\zp) = 0$ this gives $g \equiv 0$.
\end{proof}

Two further classical facts are used repeatedly and are recorded here so that each later
use can cite a statement; both are from \sscite{feller2009introduction}, Vol.~2,
\S~XIII.1: Theorem~2, p.~431, and Theorems~1 and~1a, pp.~430 and~432, the latter stated
for arbitrary measures.

% shared with blueprint prop:laplace-continuity
\begin{proposition}[continuity theorem for Laplace transforms]\label{prop:laplace-continuity}
Let $\mu_n, \mu$ be probability measures on $[0,\infty)$. Then $\mu_n \to \mu$ weakly if and
only if $\hat\mu_n(s) \to \hat\mu(s)$ for every $s > 0$.
\end{proposition}

% shared with blueprint prop:laplace-uniqueness
\begin{proposition}[uniqueness of the Laplace transform]\label{prop:laplace-uniqueness}
A measure on $[0,\infty)$ is determined by its Laplace transform: if $\hat\mu(s) = \hat\rho(s)$
for all $s$ in some interval $(a,\infty)$, then $\mu = \rho$.
\end{proposition}

The appendix compares measures --- the memory kernel $\kappa^{(x)}$, the potential kernel
$\ell^{(x)}$, and Lebesgue measure --- none of which is finite, so it needs the following
refinement, in which finiteness of the measures is replaced by convergence of the transform
at a single point.

% shared with blueprint prop:laplace-uniqueness-sigma-finite
\begin{proposition}[uniqueness without a finiteness assumption]\label{prop:laplace-uniqueness-sigma-finite}
Let $\mu, \rho$ be measures on $\RR$ carried by $[0,\infty)$, not assumed finite, and let
$s_0 \in \RR$ be such that $\hat\mu(s_0) < \infty$. If $\hat\mu(s) = \hat\rho(s)$ for every
$s \ge s_0$, then $\mu = \rho$.
\end{proposition}

% (the uniqueness propositions are positive-measure statements, while four proofs apply

\begin{corollary}[uniqueness for signed densities]\label{cor:laplace-uniqueness-signed}
Let $V$ be a measurable real function on $\RR$ vanishing a.e.\ on $(-\infty, 0)$, and let
$s_0 \in \RR$ be such that $\int_0^\infty e^{-s_0 t}\,|V(t)|\,dt < \infty$. If
$\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,V(t)\,dt = 0$ for every $s \ge s_0$, then $V = 0$ a.e.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $V = V_+ - V_-$. The measures $V_+(t)\,dt$ and $V_-(t)\,dt$ are carried by
$[0,\infty)$, their transforms are finite at $s_0$ --- each is bounded by
$\int e^{-s_0 t}|V|\,dt$ --- and the hypothesis makes them equal for every $s \ge s_0$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:laplace-uniqueness-sigma-finite} the two measures coincide, so
$V_+ = V_-$ a.e., i.e.\ $V = 0$ a.e.
\end{proof}

\emph{The causal vocabulary}, used from \S\ref{sec:signaling} on and in the appendix, is
the setting in which the family is read as an evolution in present time: a signal that
starts at time $0$, delayed and smoothed. A function $f \in X$ is \emph{causal} if it
vanishes a.e.\ on $(-\infty, 0)$; its transform
$\hat f(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st} f(t)\,dt$ is then defined for $s > 0$ with
$|\hat f(s)| \le \|f\|_1$. We write
\[
  \Xz := L^1(\RR_+),
\]
identified with the causal elements of $X$; by (A3) every $\Phi_{x,y}$ restricts to $\Xz$.
On $\Xz$ the translations $(\shift_r)_{r \ge 0}$ form the \emph{delay semigroup},
$(\shift_r f)(t) = f(t - r)\,\mathbf{1}_{t \ge r}$: positive isometries, and strongly
continuous because translation is continuous in $L^1$. The operator core used there is
\[
  \core \;:=\; \bigl\{\, f \in \Xz :\ f \text{ absolutely continuous},\ f' \in \Xz,\
  f(0) = 0 \,\bigr\}.
\]
It is cut so that its members are exactly the primitives of their derivatives,
$f = \int_0^{\cdot} f'$ with $f' \in \Xz$, hence bounded, which is what widens the Mellin
strips of \S\ref{sec:signaling}. They also lie in $\Xz$ themselves, which the other two
conditions do not imply, a primitive of an $L^1$ function being integrable only when
$\int_0^\infty f' = 0$.

\emph{Classes referred to later.} Five named subclasses appear in
\S\S\ref{sec:locality}--\ref{sec:implementation} and the appendix, always as the answer to
``which laws have this property'', never as tools; they are recorded here so that each use
can point to a definition. Two are classes of functions on $(0,\infty)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{Stieltjes} functions $\mathcal{S}$,
  \[
    f(s) = \frac{a}{s} + b + \int_{(0,\infty)} \frac{\mu(dt)}{s+t}, \qquad a, b \ge 0,
  \]
  a subclass of $\CM$ (\sscite{schilling2012bernstein}, Def.~2.1, p.~16, and Thm.~2.2,
  p.~17);
\item the \emph{complete Bernstein} functions $\CBF \subset \BF$: those whose Lévy measure
  has a completely monotone density (\sscite{schilling2012bernstein}, Def.~6.1, p.~69).
\end{itemize}
Three are classes of laws on $[0,\infty)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{self-decomposable} laws $\SDclass$, or class $L$: $\mu$ such that for
  every $b > 1$ there is a law $\rho_b$ with
  \[
    \hat\mu(s) = \hat\mu(s/b)\,\hat\rho_b(s)
  \]
  (\sscite{sato1999levy}, Def.~15.1, p.~90); they are infinitely divisible
  (\sscite{sato1999levy}, Prop.~15.5, p.~93), and their Laplace exponents are what
  \S\ref{sec:characterization} characterizes;
\item the \emph{generalized Gamma convolutions} $\GGC$: the laws with Laplace transform
  \[
    \exp\Bigl\{-\,a s - \int_{(0,\infty)} \log\bigl(1 + s/t\bigr)\,U(dt)\Bigr\}
  \]
  (\sscite{bondesson1992generalized}, \S3.1, p.~29) --- the Thorin subclass of
  \S\ref{sec:characterization} --- with $\GGC \subset \SDclass$
  (\sscite{bondesson1992generalized}, p.~30);
\item the laws with \emph{hyperbolically completely monotone} density, $\HCM$: densities
  $f$ with $f(uv)\,f(u/v)$ completely monotone in $v + v^{-1}$ for every $u > 0$
  (\sscite{bondesson1992generalized}, \S4.3, p.~55), with $\HCM \subset \GGC$
  (\sscite{bondesson1992generalized}, Thm.~4.3.1, p.~57, the regularity conditions removed
  on p.~68).
\end{itemize}
So $\HCM \subset \GGC \subset \SDclass$.

\begin{remark}[the delay dictionary]\label{rem:delay-dictionary}
Every integral formula in this paper is an instance of one physical picture, worth
fixing once. A causal kernel is the law of a \emph{random delay}: the measurement
$\Phi_{x,y}f$ reports the signal late by a random amount $T_{x,y} \sim \mu_{x,y}$,
the transform $\hat\mu_{x,y}(s) = \Ex\, e^{-sT_{x,y}}$ is the fraction of a probe
exponential surviving the stage, and the exponent $g = -\log\hat\mu$ is the
attenuation it accumulates --- additive along the cascade, as absorbances are
additive through layered media (the optical reading is developed in
Remark~\ref{rem:optical-depth}, where the analogy is made literal). In this reading
the Lévy--Khintchine form
\[
  g(s) \;=\; b_0\,s \;+\; \int_0^\infty \bigl(1 - e^{-st}\bigr)\,\nu(dt)
\]
is an inventory of the two mechanisms by which delay can accumulate.

\emph{The drift $b_0$ is deterministic latency}: a fixed transport lag, shifting
every kernel bodily; its extreme case is the pure delay family
$\mu_{0,x} = \delta_{b_0 x}$.

\emph{The measure $\nu$ is a catalogue of random delay events}: $\nu(dt)$ is the
rate, per unit of scale, at which lag increments of size $\approx t$ occur. Each
size contributes independently as a Poisson stream, and the integrand is that
stream's cost: over an infinitesimal stretch of scale $dx$, a size-$t$ event
occurs with probability $\nu(dt)\,dx$ and multiplies the transform by $e^{-st}$,
so the stage's factor is $1 - \nu(dt)\,dx\,(1 - e^{-st})$ and the exponent picks
up $(1 - e^{-st})\,\nu(dt)\,dx$. The integral is therefore literally a positive
superposition --- the catalogue lists which delays are available and how often.
(``Event'' here means an increment of the observer's accumulated \emph{lag} as
the aperture widens; the signal itself is never discontinuous.)

\emph{There is no diffusion term, and its absence is a theorem, not a choice.} On
the full line the Lévy--Khintchine formula carries a Gaussian term
$\tfrac12\sigma^2 s^2$; on the half-line it cannot appear, because a diffusing
quantity oscillates and an accumulated delay only grows. The term that generates
spatial scale space is thus structurally deleted from the time-causal axis ---
causality does not merely disfavor the Gaussian aperture, it removes its slot in
the generator. Classifying time-causal scale spaces thereby becomes classifying
delay catalogues. (Diffusion will reappear, but on the observer's memory line
rather than the signal axis: the locality theorem of \S\ref{sec:locality} closes
that ladder at the Bessel operator, an honest second-order generator over the
embodied record.)

\emph{The natural coordinate for catalogues is log-delay.} Writing $t = e^\theta$
turns the density shape the characterization singles out into a profile per unit
log-delay,
\[
  \nu(dt) = \frac{k(t)}{t}\,dt = \tilde k(\theta)\,d\theta,
  \qquad \tilde k(\theta) := k(e^\theta),
\]
since $dt/t$ is the invariant measure of the dilation group --- so \emph{dilation
acts on catalogues by translation in $\theta$}. The classification results of
\S\ref{sec:characterization} then become shape conditions on one curve
(Figure~\ref{fig:delay-catalogue}): full scale covariance forces $\tilde k$
nonincreasing --- zooming out may only add delay intensity, which is
self-decomposability; the semigroup subclass is the exactly scale-free profile
$\tilde k(\theta) = c\,e^{-\alpha\theta}$, whose refusal to decay faster
\emph{is} the heavy tail. The extreme rays are unit steps --- log-uniform bands
of delays, the Dickman exponents --- and every admissible catalogue is a unique
stack of such steps; the completely monotone profiles form the Thorin subclass
$\GGC$ above, whose finite moments correspond to a cliff-like cutoff of long
delays.
\end{remark}
